Uganda's farming runs on rain: what five analyses show

Five pieces of work, one connected story. Rainfall sets the harvest, the harvest sets prices, prices and surpluses drive trade with Uganda's neighbours, and irrigation and coffee show where that dependence can be reduced or turned into income. This page draws out what holds across all five, what decision-makers can use, and where Uganda's agricultural data falls short.

What holds across all five

  1. The second rains are the swing season, and the forecastable one. October–December rainfall varies more than March–May, follows the Indian Ocean Dipole, and can be anticipated from July–August. It also feeds the December–February maize exports. Medium · Rainfall, Trade
  2. Rain reaches prices through the harvest. A dry first season raises maize prices about 9–11% in September–December, and less green crops are followed by higher prices. Medium · Food prices
  3. The seasonal price cycle dwarfs weather shocks. Maize swings about 30% every year between harvest and lean season, so storing grain earns about 25–30% in real terms on average. High · Food prices
  4. Uganda's maize market is regional. Prices move almost in step with Kenya's and Rwanda's, and gaps halve within two months. Border shocks disrupted trade flows but did not depress Ugandan prices. High · Trade
  5. Karamoja is the outlier on every measure: the driest and most volatile rainfall, the highest dry-spell risk, the biggest seasonal price swings, and water that is hardest to reach, even as its short rains get wetter. High · all five
  6. Coffee shows what happens when farmers are tied to a world market: two-thirds of a world price change reaches robusta farmers within the month, and farmers now get 70–80% of the world robusta price. High · Coffee
  7. The weakest link is the data. Official crop statistics cannot detect droughts, trade records disagree by orders of magnitude, and coffee exports differ by 22–25% between sources. High · all five

01 · The farm year

One calendar, five analyses

Average monthly patterns from each project, on one calendar. Rain falls in two seasons in the south and one long season in the north; harvests follow; maize is cheapest after the harvests and dearest in the lean months; exports follow the harvests.

The Ugandan farm year
Darker means more · hover a cell for the value and source
ReadingMaize is dearest from April to July, when the first-season crop is still in the field, about 14% above its yearly average in June, and cheapest from October to January. Maize to Kenya peaks in December–February, after the second harvest, and is lowest in April–July, when Uganda's own prices are high. Robusta coffee earns most in June–August, arabica in February–May.
02 · The chain

From rain to harvest to price to trade

Each link in the chain was tested separately, with the confidence shown in each project.

Rain

Two seasons, one forecastable

  • No national trend since 1990, but Karamoja and the East are getting wetter, through the short rains.
  • Bad seasons are nationwide: regions rarely offset each other.
  • The two seasons are independent: a poor March–May says little about October–December.
Rainfall analysis →
Crops

Dry spells hurt the North and East

  • Crop greenness follows rain with a two-to-three-month lag and picks up the droughts of 2005, 2008–09, 2016–17 and 2021–22.
  • A two-week dry spell cuts peak greenness by about half a typical year's swing in the North and East, not in the South and West.
  • Dry-spell risk has not clearly risen since 1991.
Irrigation →
Prices

The season sets the price

  • Maize swings about 30% a year between harvest and lean season; up to 44% for sorghum in Karamoja.
  • A dry first season raises maize prices 9–11% later in the year.
  • Beans don't respond to Ugandan rainfall.
Food prices →
Trade

A regional market

  • About 490,000 tonnes of staples a year go to Kenya, South Sudan and Rwanda.
  • Prices are tied to neighbours': gaps halve within two months.
  • Kenya's 2021 ban stopped formal trade for most of a year, but prices held.

03 · The second season

The swing season is also the one that can be seen coming

Three projects point at the same months.

FindingEvidenceConfidence
October–December rain varies more than March–MayAbout 20% a year nationally against 13% for the long rains (rainfall synthesis)High
The Indian Ocean in July–August forecasts itThe July–August Dipole index, known by early September, cuts cross-validated forecast error by about 20% in Central and the Lake Victoria basin; nothing forecasts the long rainsMedium
The same signal points to next year's lean-season pricesEach +1 °C in July–August is followed by maize prices about 24% lower the next February–May (food prices; not replicated in wholesale data)Suggestive
The second harvest feeds the main export window44% of the year's maize to Kenya crosses in December–February (food trade)High
SoA September outlook for the short rains is, in effect, also an early read on the December–February export season and the following lean season's prices. That makes it useful to traders, storage operators and food-security planners, not only to farmers deciding what to plant.
04 · Karamoja

The outlier on every measure

LensWhat Karamoja showsSource
RainfallThe driest region (917 mm a year) and the most variable (annual swings about twice the national average), but getting significantly wetter: +6.7 mm a year, through the short rainsRainfall
Dry spellsMoroto has a two-week dry spell in 73% of growing windows, Kaabong 66%, Karenga 57%; under 10% across most of the South and WestIrrigation
PricesSorghum, the staple, swings up to 44% in a year; prices move with the national market, and the 2022 spike came during national shortfalls while Karamoja's own crops were near normalFood prices
WaterLittle cropland near permanent water, and basement rock with low-yielding boreholes: high need, water hard to reachIrrigation
SoKaramoja's food security is decided as much by national harvests and prices as by its own rain. Water harvesting, drought-tolerant crops and price-aware food assistance fit better than pump irrigation, and its wetting short rains may widen the window for a second crop. That last point is worth testing on the ground.
05 · Coffee

The cash crop: a world price, a warming belt and a yield gap

Prices

Farmers get the world price fast

  • Farmers' share of the world robusta price rose from about 45% after liberalisation (1992–93) to 70–80% today.
  • About two-thirds of a world price change reaches robusta farmers within the month.
  • The 2024–25 farm-gate boom was almost entirely the world price.
Coffee report →
Climate

Heat costs exports

  • Daytime highs in the robusta zones are rising 0.43–0.67 °C a decade (reanalysis; needs a station check).
  • A hot January–June cuts robusta exports by about 9–16% per standard deviation; a wet July–December lifts the next crop.
  • Arabica shows no consistent signal.
Coffee report →
Benchmark

Big, fast-growing, low-yield

  • 6th-largest producer and exporter in the world, and the fastest-growing of the top ten (5% a year).
  • Paid about 85% of the world reference for its mix, more than Vietnam.
  • Yield is perhaps a fifth of Vietnam's, but rests on imputed area.

07 · What it means

Who can use this, and how

Farmers, traders and lenders

Plan around the season

  • Storage pays: about 25–30% in real terms from harvest to lean season, before losses.
  • A September short-rains outlook is an early read on export demand and lean-season prices.
  • Coffee farmers now track the world price closely, so price risk management matters more than ever.
Government and partners

Target by place

  • Pump irrigation fits 29 districts, mostly in the East and around Lake Kyoga.
  • Karamoja needs water harvesting, drought-tolerant crops and price-aware assistance.
  • Predictable trade rules matter: a ban lifted in days still stopped formal maize trade for most of a year.
Statisticians

Fix the foundations

  • Reconcile trade records with Kenya and Rwanda.
  • Measure crop area and yield directly, starting with coffee and maize.
  • Publish district data for public irrigation and input programmes.
